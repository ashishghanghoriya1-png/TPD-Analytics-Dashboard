FIELD TRANSMISSION AUDIT Root Cause Diagnostic Analysis

Empirical Diagnostic Analysis: Why Field Practices Diverged from Targets

Unpacking transmission bottlenecks between orientation attendance and direct classroom practice across 3,099 clusters.
1. The False Comfort Trap (4.80 vs 48.0%)
Cluster meeting satisfaction surveys recorded a 4.80 out of 5.00 rating. However, direct classroom observations revealed child-safe climate practices at only 48.0%. Cluster discussions were warm and cordial, but without supervised role-play in managing learner errors constructively, teachers defaulted to traditional evaluative habits in the classroom.
2. The Multi-Grade Grouping Barrier (63.8% vs 80.0%)
While lesson closure reached 89.2% adoption, ability-based student grouping stalled at 63.8%. Field investigation shows that 68% of Madhya Pradesh primary schools operate with 2 or 3 teachers managing 5 grades simultaneously. Single-grade grouping protocols collapse when one educator must manage grades 1, 2, and 3 in a shared room.
3. Concrete Learning Material Shortage (38.8%)
38.8% of teachers reported a shortage of physical math kits, counters, and flashcards during monthly cluster sessions. When teachers cannot practice tactile routines during peer meetings, they cannot execute concrete-to-pictorial math progressions in their schools.

INTERVENTION 2 District Orientation (DO Master Trainer Cadre)
Scope: 8,459 Master Trainers | Session Interactivity Score: 1.37 / 4.0
✓ What We CAN Do (High-Impact Solutions)
  • Run physical classroom simulations: Have master trainers rehearse live demonstrations on blackboards with peer role-playing before going to clusters.

  • Provide 1-page session scripts: Replace thick training manuals with a 1-page timeline showing exact 15-minute group activities and prompt questions.
  • Assess practical delivery: Evaluate master trainers on hands-on learning tool demonstrations instead of written slide quizzes.
✕ What We CANNOT Do (Systemic Traps to Avoid)
  • Do not allow cross-cycle carryover: Never let trainers certified six months earlier lead sessions without attending the current month orientation.
  • Do not rely on slide lecture monologues: Lecturing from slides caused session interactivity to collapse to 1.37 out of 4.0 in state evaluations.
  • Do not conduct orientation without tactile kits: Training without physical kits prevents trainers from modeling hands-on tools in cluster meetings.
  • Do not treat cascading as automatic: Expecting orientation content to cascade naturally without structured observation guides leaves 42.17% of clusters without support.

ONLINE LEARNING DIAGNOSTIC Platform Transmission & Completion Mechanics

Empirical Diagnostic Analysis: Understanding Digital Drop-off and Gains

Examining pedagogical design factors that produced both double-digit score gains and steep completion cliffs.
1. Course 3 Micro-Video Success (+20.3% Gain)
Mission Ankur Course 3 achieved a +20.3% pre-to-post knowledge gain, the highest across all modules. This course replaced static text slides with 3-minute video clips showing real classroom teachers modeling place value grouping. Seeing classroom routines visually produced immediate conceptual understanding.
2. Course 4 Assessment Cliff (21.21% Completion)
Completion fell from 78.2% in Course 1 to 21.21% in Course 4. Course 4 tested multi-digit fractions with strict passing criteria but zero feedback on failed attempts. Teachers who failed twice had no diagnostic hints explaining their mistakes, leading to frustration and platform abandonment.
3. Course 6 Human Mentor Recovery (69.99% Comp / 62.04% Score)
In Course 6, the state introduced blended mentor support: Jan Shikshaks reviewed difficult assessment items during in-person cluster meetings before teachers submitted the digital quiz. This human feedback loop restored completion to 69.99% and lifted scores to 62.04%.

2. The Three Big Leaks Where Teachers Drop Out
Leak 1: The Online Course Cliff 87,725 Teachers Lost
Courses 2 to 4 in Foundational Numeracy

116,082 teachers finished Course 2. But 40,049 dropped at Course 3, and another 47,676 dropped at Course 4. Only 13,618 teachers completed Course 6.

Why it happened: Administrative follow-up dropped sharply after Course 2. Courses 3 and 4 were also longer, and teachers who failed the 70% quiz passing mark abandoned the course without simple retake guidance.

Simple Measure: Break lengthy lectures into short 5-minute segments, allow instant quiz retakes with explanations, and send completion reminder SMS messages.
Leak 2: The November Meeting Slump 8,390 Teachers Lost
Mid-Year Attendance Dip in Cluster Sessions

Cluster attendance fell from 21,958 in September to 13,568 in November. It recovered to 17,595 in December and 17,898 in January.

Why it happened: November coincides with Diwali festival vacations, harvest season, and mid-term exam duties.

Simple Measure: Reschedule the November meeting into early December or provide a short audio phone module during festival weeks.
Leak 3: The Online-Only Disconnect 143,085 Single-Channel
Teachers Missing In-Person Peer Support

Teachers who do online courses alone have a 51.9% retention rate. Teachers who participate in both online modules and in-person cluster meetings have an 88.4% retention rate.

Why it happened: Online learning without peer discussion leads to disengagement. Cluster meetings give teachers a community of practice.

Simple Measure: Send automated text messages to online course completers inviting them to their local cluster venue.

Central Square Foundation (CSF)
State Footprint: Haryana (NIPUN Haryana) & Uttar Pradesh
Governance
What they did differently: Instituted monthly governance reviews chaired directly by Deputy Commissioners (District Collectors). Produced scripted daily teacher guides with lesson steps broken down to the 10-minute mark to reduce teacher cognitive burden.
Trade-off: High administrative pressure created compliance-driven reporting where teachers hurried through steps to meet checklist criteria rather than checking student comprehension.
Takeaway for Replication: Administrative authority guarantees attendance, but must be paired with fear-free peer reflection so teachers admit what they do not understand.
STiR Education (Centre for Intrinsic Motivation)
State Footprint: Delhi, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu
Motivation
What they did differently: Centered their entire design on intrinsic teacher motivation. Built monthly Teacher Communities of Practice into official school timetables, measuring teacher autonomy, classroom curiosity, and peer recognition.
Trade-off: Kept top-down assessment and testing out of meetings to protect trust, which made it difficult to establish a direct causal link to student foundational literacy and numeracy gains.
Takeaway for Replication: Peer dialogue builds strong teacher ownership, but needs structured classroom pedagogical targets (like ability grouping and lesson synthesis) to shift learning outcomes.
Language & Learning Foundation (LLF)
State Footprint: Uttar Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Rajasthan
Language
What they did differently: Deep linguistic focus. Designed Multilingual Education (MLE) materials in tribal areas (such as Halbi and Gondi), training teachers to use home languages as a bridge to formal Hindi. Deployed external observation tools with spot-checks.
Trade-off: Highly tailored linguistic design is labor-intensive and slower to scale across diverse secondary school cadres covering science and mathematics.
Takeaway for Replication: Tribal belts in new states (such as Bastar in Chhattisgarh or Santhal Parganas in Jharkhand) require mother-tongue bridge modules rather than uniform standard language guides.
Leadership for Equity (LFE)
State Footprint: Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh
Middle-Tier
What they did differently: Embedded entirely within existing government institutions (DIETs, SCERT, Kendra Pramukhs). Refused to hire large parallel external field teams, focusing instead on rewriting administrative job descriptions and training state officials.
Trade-off: Visible field momentum is slower because delivery moves at the pace of government administrative approvals and civil service posting cycles.
Takeaway for Replication: Long-term survival requires codifying teacher training rhythms into state service rules so the system continues when project support ends.
Kaivalya Education Foundation (Piramal)
State Footprint: Bihar, Rajasthan, Aspirational Districts
Leadership
What they did differently: Deployed the Gandhi Fellowship model, placing young graduates in rural blocks to live in communities and coach school Headmasters on instructional leadership and school management.
Trade-off: High operational overhead to recruit, house, and support hundreds of external fellows across remote districts; risk of program fade once fellows graduate.
Takeaway for Replication: Strengthen the existing school Headmaster as an instructional coach rather than relying on outside contractual workers.
Madhi Foundation
State Footprint: Tamil Nadu (Ennum Ezhuthum Mission)
Digital Tracking
What they did differently: Complete digital tracking through the Palli Parvai app. Mentors receive automated weekly schedules that route them specifically to classrooms where student milestone mastery is falling behind.
Trade-off: Requires 100% device ownership and dependable connectivity; teachers report survey fatigue when daily reporting burdens become too heavy.
Takeaway for Replication: Automated mentor dispatching based on real classroom data eliminates wasted travel time and targets help where teachers need it most.
The Replication Boundary: What Stays Local vs What Can Be Exported
Attempting to copy state-specific acronyms leads to bureaucratic rejection. Successful expansion requires exporting the delivery engine while adopting the new state's administrative hierarchy.
✕ What Cannot Be Directly Copied (Local Boundary)
  • Administrative Titles: Terms like Jan Shikshak, BAC, CAC, and RSK are specific to Madhya Pradesh and mean nothing in states using ARP, CRP, or Kendra Pramukh cadres.
  • Large Lecture Workshops: Exporting physical workshops with 80 to 120 teachers guarantees low peer practice (1.37 out of 4.00) and poor classroom translation.
  • Punitive Digital Quizzes: High-stakes pass cutoffs without answer explanations trigger completion drops like Course 4 in MP (down to 21.21%).
  • Generic Dialect Materials: Standardized text without local tribal language adaptations fails in indigenous belts.
✓ The Replicable Core (The Delivery Engine)
  • Tri-Intervention Cadence: Monthly District Orientation feeding into monthly cluster workshops (Shaikshik Samwaad) backed by weekly digital micro-nudges.
  • Single-Cycle Cadre Fidelity: Strict policy that facilitators certified in DO for that month lead that month's cluster sessions, reducing decay from 18.3% down to 1.7%.
  • Room Cap at 30 to 35: Mandating smaller cluster rooms with at least 45 minutes of active peer role-play per session.
  • TabFM Risk-Based Mentoring: Triaging mentor visits using attendance drops, quiz dropouts, and classroom observation rubrics.
The 5-Step Replication Roadmap (Year 1 Blueprint)
A structured operational sequence to establish the tri-intervention system in any new state government.
1
Months 1 to 2: Middle-Tier Cadre Diagnostic & Institutional Anchoring

Review their current workload to ensure academic coaching days are formally ring-fenced by the State Education Secretary, preventing mentors from being diverted to non-academic administrative duties.
2

3
Months 5 to 6: Digital Learning Safeguards & Micro-Nudges
Build 3 to 5 minute video demonstrations focused on foundational skills. 4

Select 3 to 5 contrasting districts (one high-performing urban district, one remote rural district, and one tribal multilingual district).
